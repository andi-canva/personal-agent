# Steelman: example goal doc

_Reviewed: 2026-03-20 | Mode: standard | Perspectives: Narrative Clarity, Outcome Ownership, User Advocacy_
_This isn't a rewrite of your doc. It's a set of questions worth sitting with before you ship it._

## TLDR

- **What's strong:** your framing names a real shift in scope and signals why the goal matters now
- **What's missing:** the goal name is doing too much — it explains the mechanism when it should land the outcome. and the metric hasn't caught up with the bigger scope yet.
- **Worth sitting with:** does your goal name need to carry the scope-change story, or can a subtitle do that? and if the scope is bigger now, shouldn't the metric be too?

## At a glance

| Perspective | What's off | A different way to look at it |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative Clarity | the name explains how, not why | lead with the outcome, let the mechanism sit underneath |
| Outcome Ownership | scope grew but the metric didn't keep up | make the success condition match the ambition |
| User Advocacy | user value is implied, never stated | show what actually changes for the person using the product |

## Perspectives

### Narrative Clarity

> "Unless what you're saying is truly controversial, a ground-breaker, or new to your audience, you don't need to spend too much time elaborating on the concept itself and why it matters. People really want to know how do I do this?"

**Wes Kao**, on *Become a better communicator: Specific frameworks to improve your clarity, influence, and impact* (Lenny's Podcast)

**What's off:** Your goal name is doing two jobs — explaining the mechanism and signaling the why. That makes it defensible, but nobody walks away with a crisp sense of what changed. When someone reads the goal for the first time, they should immediately get what you're trying to achieve. Right now they get how you plan to achieve it.

**A different way to look at it:** Instead of leading with the technical mechanism, what if the goal name just said what changes for the business or the user — and let the supporting copy explain the how?

**Two ways you could go:**
- [ ] **A:** put the user or business outcome directly in the goal name
- [ ] **B:** keep the current technical signal, but add a subtitle that states the why in plain language

### Outcome Ownership

> "Most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had. If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow."

**Jeff Bezos**, quoted in *Five habits of highly annoying product managers* (Lenny's Newsletter)

**What's off:** Your scope expanded, but your success metric is still sized for the old scope. That creates a gap — the goal hints at a bigger ambition than it can currently measure. Anyone reviewing this will notice that the name promises more than the metric can verify.

**A different way to look at it:** Right now your name says one thing, your scope says another, and your metric says a third. What if they told the same story? Either size the metric up to match the scope, or be explicit that the metric is lagging on purpose.

**Two ways you could go:**
- [ ] **A:** revise the success metric to match the broader scope
- [ ] **B:** keep the current metric, but explicitly call out the gap as temporary — "we'll measure X for now while we build the instrumentation for Y"

## The takeaway

Your document is trying to do two things at once: signal a scope change and preserve continuity with what came before. That tension is making the name defensible but not crisp, and the metric honest but undersized. The most important choice here is whether you want the goal name to carry the scope-change story or whether supporting context can do that job instead. This critique would change if the technical mechanism is itself politically important to name, or if the metric can't change this quarter for reporting reasons.
